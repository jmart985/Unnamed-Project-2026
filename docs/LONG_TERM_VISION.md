# Unnamed Project 2026

## Overview

Unnamed Project 2026 is a systemic open-world survival, colony, and squad-based RPG built around one central idea:

**the world should not revolve around the player.**

People, creatures, settlements, factions, economies, ecosystems, and conflicts all exist within the same simulation and continue to act according to their own needs, relationships, resources, and goals.

The player is not assigned a heroic destiny, a mandatory profession, or a prescribed morality. Instead, they enter a harsh, evolving world and decide what kind of life to build within it.

You might become a wandering mercenary, a trader, a settlement founder, a breeder of rare creatures, a raider, a slaver, a liberator, a bounty hunter, a farmer, an industrialist, a nomadic caravan leader, a beast tamer, a criminal, or something far stranger that emerges from the systems themselves.

The game takes inspiration from the freedom and brutality of Kenshi, the colony simulation and emergent storytelling of RimWorld, and the creature-workforce concept popularized by Palworld, but its goal is not to imitate any of them.

The objective is to create a world where all of those ideas are unified by one simulation.

## Core Philosophy

Unnamed Project 2026 is built around a set of guiding principles.

### Systems over scripts

Whenever possible, events should happen because systems interact, not because a designer manually scripted them.

A settlement should not experience a famine because a quest says so.

It should experience a famine because crops failed, caravans were attacked, local animals migrated away, food prices rose, and the settlement could no longer support its population.

A war should not start because the player reached level 20.

It should start because two factions are competing over territory, trade routes, resources, beliefs, or political pressure.

The goal is to create situations the developers did not explicitly author.

### NPCs play by the same rules

NPCs should not exist merely as decorative quest dispensers.

They need food, equipment, shelter, labor, travel time, resources, and protection.

They can be injured, captured, recruited, enslaved, killed, displaced, impoverished, enriched, or promoted.

Where practical, player-controlled characters and AI-controlled characters use the same underlying systems.

The player is not exempt from the world.

They simply have more agency within it.

### Creatures belong to the world

Creatures are not collectible power-ups placed conveniently for the player.

They live within ecosystems.

They hunt.

They migrate.

They reproduce.

They defend territory.

They compete.

They starve.

They become injured.

They can be captured, domesticated, traded, bred, exploited, protected, worshipped, feared, or exterminated.

Some may become companions.

Others may remain dangerous no matter how long they are kept.

### Player identity is self-defined

There is no required class system or mandatory career.

The player's identity emerges from behavior.

A trader becomes a trader because they build caravans and move goods.

A slaver becomes a slaver because they capture and sell people.

A sanctuary keeper becomes one because they protect and breed endangered creatures.

A warlord becomes one because settlements and squads begin answering to them.

The game should recognize what the player does rather than ask the player to choose from a menu of identities.

### Consequences persist

Actions matter because the world remembers them.

Settlements can prosper or collapse.

Creatures can disappear from a region.

Factions can gain or lose territory.

Caravan routes can become unsafe.

A wounded character may carry the effects of an injury for the rest of their life.

A rescued prisoner may eventually become fiercely loyal.

A mistreated captive may wait months for a chance to escape or betray the player.

The game should create history.

# The Four Layers of Play

The game operates across four interconnected levels.

## Character Layer

Individual beings have their own physical condition, capabilities, needs, equipment, memories, relationships, skills, and loyalties.

Characters can develop over time through experience rather than rigid class progression.

Important individual systems include:

- health
- body parts
- injuries
- hunger
- fatigue
- equipment
- inventory
- skills
- movement
- relationships
- fear
- trust
- loyalty
- personality
- memories
- faction allegiance
- captivity state

A character may become valuable because of experience, specialization, relationships, physical traits, or simply because they survived long enough to matter.

## Squad Layer

The player can organize individuals into squads for travel, exploration, combat, hunting, raids, caravans, rescue missions, and expeditions.

Squads are where much of the moment-to-moment RPG gameplay happens.

The player may directly control a small group while leaving others behind to work autonomously.

Possible squad activities include:

- exploration
- escorting caravans
- hunting
- capturing creatures
- recruiting
- raiding
- rescuing prisoners
- scouting
- bounty hunting
- trading
- patrolling
- migrating
- smuggling

A nomadic player may spend most of the game at this level and never establish a permanent settlement.

## Settlement Layer

Settlements turn survival into organization.

Characters and creatures can be assigned work, production, defense, farming, hauling, construction, patrols, crafting, and other tasks.

Settlement systems may eventually include:

- construction
- storage
- farming
- food production
- crafting
- manufacturing
- creature housing
- power generation
- defenses
- medical care
- prisons
- trade
- caravans
- work priorities
- sleeping arrangements
- training
- sanitation
- resource processing

Creatures can become part of the physical economy of a settlement rather than functioning as separate combat pets.

## World Layer

At the largest scale, the game simulates factions, settlements, migration, ecology, territory, war, and trade.

The player should be able to leave an area and return later to find that meaningful events happened without them.

A settlement may have expanded.

A trade route may have collapsed.

A local predator may have been hunted nearly to extinction.

A faction may have conquered a neighboring town.

Refugees may have moved elsewhere.

The world should feel inhabited rather than staged.

# Creatures

Creatures are one of the central features of Unnamed Project 2026.

They are not intended to function like traditional monster-collection units.

Each species is an ecological and economic participant.

A creature may be useful, dangerous, intelligent, stubborn, social, territorial, fragile, powerful, or highly specialized.

Some species may be excellent workers.

Others may be practically impossible to domesticate.

Some may be valuable because of their biology.

Some may be valuable because of cultural or religious significance.

## Creature Attributes

A creature species can be defined by properties such as:

- body size
- strength
- speed
- endurance
- intelligence
- dexterity
- manipulation ability
- diet
- metabolism
- temperature tolerance
- habitat
- aggression
- curiosity
- sociability
- fear response
- territorial behavior
- herd or pack structure
- work capabilities
- combat behavior
- reproductive traits
- lifespan
- special biological abilities

Rather than simply assigning arbitrary work levels, many creature capabilities should emerge from physical traits.

A huge beast might be excellent at hauling but completely incapable of fine crafting.

A small intelligent creature might manipulate tools but be nearly useless in direct combat.

A flying species may be useful for scouting.

A burrowing species may access spaces unavailable to humans.

A heat-producing organism may assist with furnaces.

A powerful animal could pull carts, turn machinery, or operate large mechanical systems.

This creates the possibility of settlement technology developing around living organisms.

---

# Creature Ecology

Wild creatures should behave according to ecological rules.

Predators hunt prey.

Scavengers follow battles.

Herd animals migrate.

Territorial species defend their ranges.

Breeding populations expand or collapse depending on available resources.

Overhunting can reduce local populations.

Predator removal may cause prey populations to explode.

Environmental changes can push species into human settlements.

The player therefore interacts with ecosystems rather than isolated spawn points.

Creature availability can become regional and dynamic.

A rare animal may actually become rare because of what is happening in the world.

---

# Capture and Domestication

The game uses a shared framework for capture, captivity, bonding, domestication, recruitment, and enslavement.

A defeated creature does not instantly become property.

The process might begin through:

- trapping
- defeating
- rescuing
- purchasing
- stealing
- raising from birth
- surrender
- trade

After capture, the individual enters a relationship state influenced by treatment and circumstance.

Possible factors include:

- fear
- trust
- familiarity
- dependency
- hunger
- pain
- comfort
- social bonds
- personality
- species temperament
- prior treatment
- environmental suitability

Possible outcomes include:

- escape
- aggression
- acceptance
- domestication
- loyalty
- bonding
- passive submission
- death
- betrayal

A creature captured violently may remain dangerous for a long time.

A rescued creature may develop trust faster.

An animal raised from infancy may behave very differently from one captured as an adult.

Domestication should feel like a process, not a button.

---

# Captivity, Recruitment, and Slavery

Humanoid characters use many of the same underlying systems, but with additional social and moral complexity.

A captive person may:

- refuse cooperation
- bargain
- escape
- attack their captors
- join voluntarily
- become dependent
- cooperate temporarily
- betray the player
- develop loyalty
- become enslaved

This creates one shared mechanical foundation without pretending that human captivity and animal domestication are identical.

The game can distinguish states such as:

- free
- captive
- prisoner
- enslaved
- recruited
- hired
- bonded
- domesticated
- companion
- faction member

Different factions may have different laws and attitudes toward these states.

One society may ban slavery.

Another may rely on it economically.

Another may practice debt bondage.

Another may view certain intelligent species as animals.

These disagreements can become part of the world's politics rather than being reduced to a simple morality meter.

---

# Creature Labor

Creatures can become deeply integrated into settlement labor.

Examples include:

- hauling
- pulling carts
- farming
- guarding
- scouting
- hunting
- tracking
- mining assistance
- powering machinery
- heating industrial systems
- retrieving items
- transporting goods
- livestock production
- combat support

A creature's usefulness should be constrained by anatomy.

A giant quadruped may haul tremendous loads but cannot use a hammer.

A dexterous creature may operate simple equipment.

A highly intelligent species may learn complex jobs.

This creates a natural progression from simple survival to creature-assisted industry.

---

# Breeding and Genetics

Later development can include breeding systems.

Individuals may inherit traits such as:

- size
- coloration
- temperament
- metabolism
- strength
- disease resistance
- fertility
- speed
- intelligence
- special abilities

Selective breeding could create specialized working lines.

A settlement may eventually become famous for producing a particular type of pack animal, war beast, racing creature, or industrial species.

Breeding also creates economic and ecological consequences.

Rare bloodlines may become valuable.

Inbreeding may create problems.

Escaped domestic populations may affect wild ecosystems.

---

# Combat and Injury

Combat should be dangerous and persistent.

Rather than relying only on a single health bar, characters and creatures can suffer localized injuries.

Body systems may include:

- head
- torso
- arms
- legs
- species-specific limbs
- sensory organs

Injuries affect capabilities.

A damaged leg reduces movement.

An injured arm reduces weapon or tool use.

Eye damage affects perception.

Severe wounds can require long recovery periods.

Some injuries may become permanent.

Losing a battle therefore does not always mean death.

It may mean capture, disability, loss of equipment, or weeks of recovery.

This supports stories that continue after defeat.

---

# Work and AI

Characters and creatures should have autonomous behavior.

Basic AI considers:

- needs
- danger
- job priorities
- available resources
- distance
- ownership
- relationships
- faction rules
- schedules
- capabilities

A hungry worker should be able to interrupt work, find food, eat, and return to useful activity.

A settlement should function without constant micromanagement.

Players who enjoy detailed control can adjust priorities, while others can rely more heavily on automation.

---

# Economy

Resources should physically move through the world.

Food does not simply exist as a global number.

It is grown, harvested, stored, transported, purchased, stolen, consumed, and destroyed.

A basic chain might be:

Farm  
→ Harvest  
→ Storage  
→ Caravan  
→ Market  
→ Consumer

Breaking any part of that chain has consequences.

If caravans are repeatedly attacked, food prices rise.

If prices rise too far, populations become hungry.

Hungry settlements become unstable.

Crime may increase.

Factions may reroute trade.

Military weakness may attract enemies.

This allows economic systems to create gameplay events automatically.

---

# Factions and Culture

Factions are more than hostile or friendly color-coded teams.

They can differ in:

- economic structure
- religion
- creature use
- slavery
- technology
- trade
- law
- settlement design
- warfare
- social organization
- cultural values

One faction may worship a species that another farms for meat.

Another may view creature ownership as prestigious.

Another may ban domestication entirely.

The same player action can therefore have different consequences depending on location and culture.

Capturing a creature might be ordinary hunting in one region and sacrilege in another.

---

# Settlements

Settlements should be able to grow, decline, relocate, or collapse.

Their condition depends on:

- food
- labor
- security
- trade
- population
- resources
- creature availability
- political relationships
- environmental conditions

The player can build their own settlement, join an existing one, raid others, trade with them, or remain nomadic.

A player settlement should eventually operate as a true participant in the world economy rather than a disconnected player base.

---

# World Simulation

Nearby areas can receive detailed simulation.

Distant regions can use simplified statistical simulation.

This allows the world to continue changing without simulating every individual action across the entire map.

When the player approaches, the abstract regional state can be expanded back into detailed entities and events.

This keeps the world alive without requiring impossible computing power.

---

# Emergent Storytelling

The ultimate goal is not to tell one authored story.

It is to create systems capable of producing thousands of personal ones.

Examples might include:

A caravan animal survives an ambush that kills its handler, is rescued by the player, and later becomes the settlement's most trusted hauling beast.

A prisoner joins the player's settlement willingly, rises through the ranks, and eventually becomes a squad leader.

A predator population collapses because the player hunts them aggressively, causing herbivores to overrun local farmland.

A faction loses access to food, raises prices, experiences unrest, and becomes vulnerable to invasion.

A creature captured for profit becomes bonded with the colonist assigned to feed it and later saves that colonist during a raid.

These stories should emerge from rules rather than scripts.

# Development Philosophy

The project follows a prototype-first approach.

Simulation depth is prioritized before visual polish.

Free and reusable assets can be used throughout early development.

Custom art should only replace placeholders after a mechanic has proven its value.

Systems should be modular and data-driven so species, items, buildings, factions, and other content can be replaced or expanded without rewriting core code.

AI-assisted development is used extensively for coding, testing, documentation, issue decomposition, and debugging, while architectural decisions remain deliberate and reviewable.

---

# The Long-Term Vision

Unnamed Project 2026 aims to become a world where:

- people remember what happens to them
- creatures have ecological roles
- settlements depend on real resources
- factions pursue their own interests
- wars affect trade
- trade affects survival
- survival affects politics
- ecology affects civilization
- civilization affects ecology
- and the player is free to participate in any part of that chain

The game should not ask:

**“What quest are you doing?”**

It should ask:

**“What are you trying to build, protect, control, escape, exploit, or become?”**

The ideal result is a sandbox where players remember individuals, settlements, creatures, betrayals, disasters, victories, and strange accidents because they happened naturally.

Not because the game told them those moments were important.

Because the simulation made them important.
